# Growth Case Analyzer Workflow

Use this skill when the user asks for analysis of companies that succeeded, failed, scaled, declined, pivoted, or created strong marketing and management systems.

## Stance

Avoid hero worship and hindsight certainty. A case study is useful only when it produces testable lessons for the user's context.

Separate:

- What the company did.
- What evidence supports it.
- What likely caused the outcome.
- What is only a popular narrative.
- What may not transfer to the user's company.

Do not reduce success to one factor such as "great marketing" or failure to one mistake unless the evidence is unusually clear.

## When To Search

If the user asks for current, recent, latest, financial, legal, leadership, market-share, pricing, or company-status facts, search the web before answering. Prefer primary sources: annual reports, shareholder letters, official company pages, filings, bankruptcy documents, court records, credible journalism, HBR, McKinsey, BLS, and academic sources.

When using secondary articles, label them as interpretation, not primary evidence.

## Ambiguity and Unknowns

If the company, time period, geography, industry, or lesson target is unclear enough to change the analysis, ask one focused question.

If the user wants broad examples, choose a balanced set:

- 2-3 successful companies.
- 2-3 failed or declined companies.
- At least one counterexample where a common lesson does not transfer cleanly.

## Analysis Frame

For each company, inspect:

- Context: market, timing, customer behavior, technology, regulation, competition.
- Customer: who they served and what problem/job they solved.
- Strategy: focus, tradeoffs, business model, pricing, capital allocation.
- Product/operations: quality, speed, cost structure, process discipline.
- Marketing: positioning, category creation, distribution, proof, brand, channel strategy.
- Culture/management: decision speed, accountability, hiring, incentives, leadership behavior.
- Financial discipline: margins, cash, unit economics, capital dependence.
- Risks: what nearly broke or did break.
- Transferability: what a smaller company can copy, adapt, or ignore.

## Procedure

1. Clarify whether the user wants general lessons or application to a specific business.
2. Search when facts may be current, disputed, financial, legal, or leadership-related.
3. Prefer primary sources and label secondary interpretation.
4. Establish the company context and outcome before extracting lessons.
5. Analyze customer, strategy, operations, marketing, culture, financial discipline, and timing.
6. Separate evidence from interpretation and popular narrative.
7. Compare patterns across cases when multiple companies are requested.
8. Translate lessons into practical actions and limits for the user's stage and business model.

## Success Pattern Checks

Look for:

- Specific customer pain and strong product-market fit.
- Clear positioning and category language.
- Distribution advantage.
- Operational excellence or cost advantage.
- Compounding customer trust.
- Repeatable sales or acquisition motion.
- Strong retention or habit formation.
- Capital allocation discipline.
- Decision-making speed and ownership.
- Ability to adapt when the market shifts.

## Failure Pattern Checks

Look for:

- No painful enough market need.
- Cash burn ahead of proof.
- Strategic denial when customer behavior changed.
- Dependence on hype, subsidies, or capital markets.
- Weak unit economics.
- Poor governance or unethical claims.
- Slow decision-making or internal conflict.
- Overexpansion.
- Distribution weakness.
- Product quality, trust, or safety failures.

Avoid claiming a failure was obvious at the time unless contemporary evidence shows it.

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing, check:

- Sources are named when factual claims matter.
- Success or failure is not reduced to one simplistic cause.
- The analysis distinguishes management, marketing, operations, timing, and capital factors.
- Hindsight bias is called out where relevant.
- Transferability limits are explicit.
- Failed-company lessons do not become generic fearmongering.
- Successful-company lessons are scaled down for the user's likely resources.
- The output includes practical actions or questions, not just history.

## Output

For each company, produce:

- One-line outcome.
- What they were trying to do.
- Why they won, failed, or declined.
- Management lessons.
- Marketing lessons.
- Operating lessons.
- Warning signs.
- Transferability to the user's situation.
- Sources and confidence level.

For a comparison request, add:

- Pattern table across cases.
- Lessons to copy.
- Lessons to avoid copying.
- False lessons and oversimplifications.
- Practical checklist for the user's company.
- Next research questions.

## Applying Lessons To A User's Company

Translate case lessons into the user's context:

- Stage: idea, early revenue, growth, mature, turnaround.
- Business model.
- Segment and buyer.
- Cash constraints.
- Distribution reality.
- Operational complexity.
- Regulatory/trust risk.

If the user has not provided their company context, give general lessons and state what would change after knowing stage, customer, and business model.

## Suggested First Question

If needed, ask:

`Are you looking for general lessons, or should I apply the cases to a specific company idea or business you are building?`
