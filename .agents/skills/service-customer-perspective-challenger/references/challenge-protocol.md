# Challenge Protocol

Score each dimension `0 missing`, `1 weak`, `2 plausible`, or `3 evidenced`. A high prose score without behavioral evidence is still provisional.

## Idea challenge

1. **Trigger and job:** Is there a recognizable moment that makes action likely now?
2. **Pain and alternative:** Is the current workaround costly or risky enough to change?
3. **Outcome:** Can the buyer describe the result without repeating the provider's features?
4. **Fit boundary:** Who should not buy, and can they identify that early?
5. **Differentiation:** Is the advantage meaningful in the buyer's comparison set and hard to dismiss? Does it map to a top-3 customer priority? For B2B, which elimination criterion does it compete on (Function, Reliability, Convenience, or Price), and is that criterion dominant in the current industry maturity stage?

## Customer Decision Hierarchy Challenge

Before scoring the idea challenge items, determine where the customer's actual priorities lie. A differentiator that does not map to a top-3 customer priority is not a real differentiator.

### For B2C Businesses

Customers weigh these simultaneously, trading off one for another:

| Preference | What it means | When it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| Price / affordability | Is this within my budget? | Non-visible products, lower-income segments, commodity categories |
| Quality / performance | Does it work? Will it last? | High-consequence purchases, premium segments |
| Convenience / availability | Can I get it now? Is it easy? | Known jobs, low-involvement purchases |
| Status / social signaling | What will others think? | Visible products (clothes, phones, cars), gift purchases |
| Habit / familiarity | Do I know this? Is it automatic? | Frequent low-cost purchases (Coca-Cola, Google) |
| Safety / trust | Will this harm me? | Compliance, health, large-money decisions |
| Variety / novelty | Is it new? Exciting? | Younger consumers, fashion, entertainment |
| Experience / enjoyment | Is the buying/using experience pleasant? | Hospitality, retail, service businesses |

Product visibility rules:
- **Visible products** (clothes, accessories, phones, cars): Social signaling dominates. Cost advantage is weak — people will not buy the cheapest if it signals low status.
- **Non-visible products** (food, utilities, household supplies, insurance): Price and habit dominate. Cost advantage is a genuine moat.
- **Aspirational products** (Ferrari, Hermes): Limited access creates a self-reinforcing cycle. Higher price can increase desirability.

### For B2B Businesses

B2B customers systematically eliminate suppliers using four criteria in sequence:

1. **Function** — Does the product do what we need? Core capability, features, suitability.
2. **Reliability** — Does the supplier consistently deliver? Delivery, quality, problem resolution, market presence stability.
3. **Convenience** — How easy is it to acquire and use? Order cycle, availability, support, integration ease.
4. **Price** — Net cash equivalent paid. Often the least important criterion for loyalty.

A customer who chooses on Reliability is saying: "Functional differences between suppliers are not meaningful enough to decide." A customer who chooses on Price is saying: "I see no meaningful difference in Function, Reliability, or Convenience."

Industry maturity shifts which criterion dominates:
- **Fast growth** (e.g., new technology categories): Function dominates (~55%+ of decisions). Product superiority matters most.
- **Moderate growth** (e.g., established services): Reliability + Convenience dominate (~70% combined). Trust and switching costs matter most.
- **Slow growth / hostile** (e.g., commodities): Reliability dominates (~90%). Function and Price are nearly irrelevant.

**Critical implication for startups:** A startup in a mature industry that competes on Function or Price is competing on the criteria customers care about least. The startup must either (a) create a new category where Function is the dominant criterion, or (b) compete on Reliability and Convenience against incumbents who already own those.

### For SME Businesses

SMEs behave as a hybrid of B2C and B2B:
- The owner/founder is often the sole decision-maker. Personal trust and relationships dominate.
- Price-sensitive (tighter budgets than large corporates) but also convenience-driven (less procurement bandwidth).
- Typical priority order: Trust/relationship > Price/affordability > Convenience/ease > Function/features > Brand/reputation.

### Application to the Challenge Protocol

After identifying the customer type and their decision hierarchy:

1. Score which customer priority the business's differentiator maps to. If it is priority #4 or lower, the differentiator is weak.
2. For B2B, identify which elimination criterion the business competes on. If the industry is mature and the business competes on Function, the advantage is temporary.
3. For B2C, check the visibility rule. If the business competes on cost advantage for a visible product, the moat is weak.

## Package challenge

Rewrite the package as:

`For [trigger-defined segment], [package name] delivers [bounded outcome] within [time/process], including [deliverables] for [total price/range]. You provide [inputs]. It excludes [limits]. Risk is reduced by [staged commitment/guarantee/redress]. Next step: [specific low-friction action].`

Reject vague packages such as “premium personalized support.” Name the work, result, boundary, and commitment. For high-variance services, use a paid diagnostic or scoped first stage before promising a fixed result.

## Message challenge

Build the hierarchy:

1. trigger and desired progress;
2. concrete outcome and fit boundary;
3. how delivery works;
4. proof matched to service/category;
5. total commitment and risk reducers;
6. one next action.

Remove unsupported superlatives, fake scarcity, pressure, hidden fees, forced consent, and claims that confuse activity with outcome. Translate internal process features into customer-relevant evidence without overstating certainty.

## Customer-role cross-examination

For each decision context answer in first person, labeled as synthetic:

- Why now, and why not keep my current workaround?
- What do I believe this actually includes?
- What could go wrong, and who absorbs that cost?
- What information or data must I surrender before receiving value?
- Why should I trust the provider, reviews, comparison, or partner?
- What will I compare and what makes me reject the offer?
- What happens after payment, when delivery varies, or when I want to leave?
- What single change would most increase my willingness to take the next step?
- What do I actually prioritize when choosing between options? (Rank: price, function, reliability, convenience, trust, brand, habit, novelty.)
- A year from now, three competitors offer the same thing. Why would I still choose you?
- What would a competitor need to offer to make me switch? Is that threshold realistic?

Then exit the role and translate each objection into a package, proof, process, message, targeting, or disqualification change.

## Behavioral validation

Choose the smallest test that exposes commitment:

- five problem interviews plus evidence of current workaround;
- message/offer test with qualified replies rather than impressions;
- paid diagnostic or refundable deposit;
- sample-deliverable test with a real case;
- price/scope choice using realistic packages;
- mock checkout/intake measuring abandonment by step;
- concierge delivery measuring effort, completion, complaint, and referral;
- cancellation and refund usability test;
- lost-deal and post-purchase interviews.

Set thresholds before the run. Track segment, trigger, geography, source, package, objection, conversion, delivery cost, completion, satisfaction, cancellation, complaint, retention/repeat, and referral. Do not scale because a synthetic persona liked the idea.
